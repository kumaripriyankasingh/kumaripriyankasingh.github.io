# Fifteen Years of Corporate Life: What It Gave, and What It Quietly Took

It has been fifteen years. In 2011, I walked out of college in Jamshedpur and into the corporate world, not really knowing what it would give me or what it would ask of me in return.

I'm writing this to look back honestly. What did I learn? Who did I meet? And what would I tell someone standing where I stood in 2011? I'll try not to be diplomatic, because I think the shiny version of corporate life is already well advertised.

## The mentor I got lucky with

Technically, my career began with a brief six months at Tally Solutions. But the place where corporate life truly began for me was Samsung in Bangalore, and that is where I met my first mentor. I genuinely could not have asked for a better one, and I still thank my luck for it.

He was brilliant technically, but that is not why I call him wonderful. He was simply one of the finest human beings I have met, then or since. From him I learned professionalism. I learned ethics. And, quietly, by watching him, I learned how to be a good person.

More than a mentor, he was someone who understood me, and that bond ended up setting the tone for the whole team. We all clicked. We had our daily tea chat break, where we talked about everything from life to office politics. Back then, it felt like these friendships would last for decades.

## How a good group quietly dissolves

Within three years, we had all left, one by one, and the group broke up.

We did what everyone does. We made a WhatsApp group. For a while we chatted, sent festival wishes, promised to meet. Then it just faded. Nobody noticed exactly when, and nobody made the effort to pull it back. New friends came into our lives, work took up the rest, and there was no room left for the old ones.

That included my mentor. I had hoped he would be part of my life forever. Today, sadly, we are not in touch.

I often compare this with my father's life. He was in a government job in a small town. He went to office at ten, came home at five, and then met his friends and neighbours over evening tea. Every single day. It was as ordinary as brushing your teeth, and they all looked forward to it.

Corporate life plus Bangalore traffic doesn't allow that. After work, meeting people takes real effort. And after a punishing week, most people just want to rest on the weekend, which is completely fair. So unless your team itself makes time for each other, your social life is mostly your office. The moment you leave the office, you often leave the people too.

## The same story, every company

That first team wasn't an exception. The same thing happened at every company I moved to. You meet people for a brief stint, you genuinely become friends, and when one of you leaves, the friendship usually leaves too.

Life stages make it harder. Straight out of college, you have three or four free years. Then people start getting married and have a second life to manage outside the office. Then kids arrive, and free time drops to almost zero.

What survives are the zero-maintenance friendships, the very old friends you can meet after a year and it feels like nothing changed. New office friendships rarely get the time to become that.

I met some truly wonderful people along the way, people I wanted in my life for good. I couldn't keep them. The blame is shared, mine as much as theirs. Eventually I understood that this is simply how it works, and I made peace with it. In corporate life, work comes first, and almost everything else takes a back seat.

## What it gave me in return

If corporate life took some people from me, it gave back a great deal too.

First, financial independence, the kind my family and I could never have imagined when I left Jamshedpur. Today I'm comfortable. I live a decent, dignified life, and that dignity comes from money earned honestly, through my own work.

It also showed me the world. Work took me to South Korea and Hong Kong, and I lived in the United States for five years. I doubt I'd have seen any of these places otherwise. Living abroad gave me an exposure to other cultures that I could never have got from reading about them.

And it kept me growing. I've worked on almost everything a software engineer can work on, and I've made a point of staying on top of every shift in tech. Today my days are full of AI, and I'm genuinely happy that I kept upgrading myself instead of getting left behind.

## What five years in America taught me

I had lived on my own since my first job, but moving to the US meant starting life from scratch with no one to help. Building a decent life there gave me a confidence I didn't have before. Today I know I can live in any part of the world, even where I have no friends, and be just fine. I love seeing how other people live, I adapt easily, and I don't need much.

A few lessons stayed with me:

  - **Be emotionally independent.** You can't build your life around depending on other people. Learning that there made the drifting friendships back home easier to accept.
  - **Live minimally.** Starting over in a new country taught me not to get attached to things. Keep life light, and be ready to move.
  - **Spend money to buy back time.** Americans readily pay for things that save time or make life better. As Indians, we often hesitate to spend, even when it costs us hours. I learned that if money gives you back time, it's an investment, not an expense.
  - **Do what you love, and put your heart in it.** Many people I met there chose work they loved and looked after themselves, yet still thought about others. Their integrity was high, and whatever they did, they did fully.

## Everyone here is smart

The competition is fierce, and I mean that. The people who make it into corporate jobs have usually worked hard their whole lives, to become financially stable and give their families a better life. Hard work is their habit. So once you're in, you're surrounded by smart, driven people, and just doing decently takes serious effort.

In a government job, growth often depends as much on your relationships as your performance. In corporate, it depends on performance. Relationships matter too, but even those are mostly built through work.

So there's a quiet trade-off. If you put more into relationships and less into work, you fall behind. At some point you start to feel like you're losing, and nobody wants that. So most people choose to compete, and friendships are what give way.

## An honest note about being single

I'm single, and almost all my former colleagues are married with kids. That adds its own layer. Even when we'd both like to catch up, I'm not really the right fit for their weekends anymore. Families naturally want to meet other families, so their children have company to play with, and that's completely understandable. So I stepped back, and over time I just went with the flow.

To be clear, I'm not writing this because I feel lonely. I've learned to live alone, and honestly, I like it. It's not that I can't live without those people. I just wish the wonderful ones were still around, still friends, still people I'd be excited to meet on a weekend.

## If you're just starting out

This is the real picture I wish someone had given me in 2011.

  - **The money is real.** If you work hard and stay consistent, corporate life pays well, and it can open up the world to you.
  - **So is the cost.** Family time and relationships will take a back seat. If you truly want to grow, expect to be lonely sometimes, and learn to be okay with it.
  - **Don't make people your escape.** You'll like some colleagues and want to spend time with them. They may be free once or twice, but not most of the time. That's not personal; it's the life everyone is juggling.
  - **Don't hold grudges.** If you get hurt by people drifting away, the resentment will only hurt you. Nobody else.

Fifteen years in, I don't regret the path. I met brilliant people, learned from the best, saw the world, and built a life I'm proud of. I just wish I'd walked in with realistic expectations. So walk in with yours.

As for me, I'm not done. I love working in tech, I love learning new things, and I want to keep contributing back to the field that gave me so much. I honestly have no complaints about the work. Here's to the next chapter.
